Mix today, hear tomorrow

Your ears are the one tool in the studio you can’t replace. This short guide is for engineers and musicians, and it starts with a question: how loud is too loud, and for how long?

85 dBA Safe time per day: 8 hours

Based on the NIOSH (1998) limit of 85 dBA for 8 hours. Every 3 dB increase halves the safe time.

How the ear works

Sound travels through three zones before your brain hears it as music.

  1. Outer ear. The pinna and ear canal funnel pressure waves to the eardrum. The canal boosts frequencies around 2–4 kHz, which is why our hearing is so sensitive to speech and to harshness in a mix.
  2. Middle ear. The eardrum moves three tiny bones (the ossicles), which act as a lever and match the air’s low pressure to the fluid of the inner ear.
  3. Inner ear. In the cochlea, the basilar membrane sorts frequencies by position. Rows of hair cells turn that movement into nerve signals. Humans do not regrow these hair cells once they are damaged.

What your hearing can and can’t do

Frequency range
Young, healthy ears hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The top end shrinks with age and exposure, so a mix engineer’s high frequencies are usually the first thing to go.
Dynamic range
From the threshold of hearing (about 0 dB SPL) to the threshold of pain (somewhere around 120–130 dB SPL) is a range of well over 100 dB. Damage starts long before pain does.
Equal loudness
Fletcher and Munson (1933) showed that we hear mid frequencies louder than bass or treble at the same level, and that difference shrinks as things get louder. Mixing too quietly or too loudly will fool your judgement of balance, and loud mixing also costs you hearing.

What goes wrong

Temporary threshold shift
That dull, muffled feeling after a loud gig. Your hearing threshold has been pushed up. It usually recovers, but repeating it is how permanent loss starts.
Noise-induced hearing loss
Permanent damage to hair cells, typically showing first around 3–6 kHz. It is painless and gradual, so most people notice it late.
Tinnitus and hyperacusis
Ringing in the ears, or everyday sounds feeling painfully loud. Both can be permanent and both are common in musicians.

Safe listening by setting

SettingWhat to do
StudioMix at a moderate level, around 70–80 dB SPL at the listening position, with short louder checks. Take a 10 minute break each hour. Check the mix on headphones at low volume too.
LiveWear musician’s earplugs with flat attenuation, so music stays clear but quieter. Stay away from the PA, and give your ears a quiet recovery period after the show.
Personal listeningKeep headphones at about 60% volume or lower. If someone next to you can hear it, or you can’t hear them, it’s too loud.

Using a dB meter or app

  1. Set the weighting. Use A-weighting (dBA) for exposure limits, because it follows the ear’s reduced sensitivity to bass. Use C-weighting (dBC) to see how much low-frequency energy is present.
  2. Use the slow response for steady noise and fast for changing material.
  3. Hold the phone away from your body, with the microphone pointing at the source, and measure for at least 30 seconds.
  4. Write down the average and the peak. Treat phone apps as a guide only: they are not calibrated like a class 1 or 2 meter.

Case study: the rehearsal room

A four-piece band rehearses for three hours, three nights a week. A meter app reads an average of 100 dBA at the drum kit. Using the calculator above, how long is the safe daily limit, and what could the band change first?

At 100 dBA the limit is about 15 minutes. Quick wins: turn the amps down so the drums stop competing, move the kit and the singer away from walls, wear earplugs, and schedule 10 minute silent breaks.
