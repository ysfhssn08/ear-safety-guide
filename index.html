Mix today, hear tomorrow

Your ears are the one tool in the studio you can’t replace. This guide is for engineers and musicians, and it starts with a question: how loud is too loud, and for how long?

How the ear works

  1. Outer ear. The pinna and canal funnel pressure waves to the eardrum and boost frequencies around 2–4 kHz.
  2. Middle ear. The three ossicles act as a lever and match the air’s low pressure to the fluid of the inner ear.
  3. Inner ear. The basilar membrane in the cochlea sorts frequencies by position, and hair cells turn that movement into nerve signals. Human hair cells do not regrow once damaged.

What your hearing can and can’t do

Frequency range
Young, healthy ears hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The top end shrinks with age and exposure.
Dynamic range
From the threshold of hearing (about 0 dB SPL) to the threshold of pain (around 120–130 dB SPL) is well over 100 dB. Damage starts long before pain does.
Equal loudness
We hear mid frequencies louder than bass or treble at the same level, and the gap shrinks as things get louder (Fletcher & Munson, 1933; International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2003).

Where is the sound coming from?

Localisation uses two main cues from having two ears (Moore, 2012). Interaural time difference is the tiny delay, up to about 0.7 ms, between a sound reaching one ear and then the other. It works best below roughly 1.5 kHz. Interaural level difference is the loudness difference caused by the head shadowing the far ear, and it matters most at higher frequencies. The shape of the pinna adds cues for height and front-back position.

For mixing, this explains why low bass is hard to place (so it is usually kept central), why panning relies on level differences, and why a mix can sound different on headphones, where each ear hears only one channel.

What goes wrong

The World Health Organization (WHO, 2015) estimates that over a billion teenagers and young adults are at risk of hearing loss from unsafe listening.

Temporary threshold shift
That muffled feeling after a loud gig. It usually recovers, but repeating it is how permanent loss starts.
Noise-induced hearing loss
Permanent hair cell damage, typically showing first around 3–6 kHz. It is painless and gradual.
Tinnitus and hyperacusis
Ringing in the ears, or everyday sounds feeling painfully loud. Both can be permanent.

Safe listening by setting

SettingWhat to do
StudioMix at a moderate level, around 70–80 dB SPL at your listening position, with short louder checks. Take a 10 minute break each hour, and check the mix on headphones at low volume.
LiveWear musician’s earplugs with flat attenuation. Stay away from the PA, and give your ears a quiet recovery period after.
Personal listeningKeep headphones at about 60% volume or lower. If someone next to you can hear it, or you can’t hear them, it’s too loud.

Measuring with a dB meter, app or analyser

  1. Set the weighting. Use A-weighting (dBA) for exposure limits. Use C-weighting (dBC) to see how much low-frequency energy is present (International Electrotechnical Commission [IEC], 2013).
  2. Use the slow response for steady noise and fast for changing material.
  3. Hold the phone at ear height, microphone towards the source, and measure for at least 30 seconds.
  4. Note the average and the peak. Phone apps are a guide only, because they are not calibrated like a class 1 or 2 meter.
  5. Load a recording into a frequency analyser (a DAW meter, or a free plug-in such as Voxengo SPAN) to see which frequencies carry the energy. A spectrogram shows how that changes over time.

Case study: the rehearsal room

A four-piece band rehearses for three hours, three nights a week. A meter app reads an average of 100 dBA at the drum kit. Using the calculator above, how long is the safe daily limit, and what could the band change first?

At 100 dBA the limit is about 15 minutes. Quick wins: turn the amps down so the drums stop competing, move the kit and singer away from walls, wear earplugs, and schedule silent breaks.
